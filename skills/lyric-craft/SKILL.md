# Lyric Craft

This is a working manual, not an appreciation course. Every principle here is a rule you can apply to a draft in front of you, and most of them tell you what to cut. The premise: great lyrics are not inspired into existence, they are revised into existence. The draft is raw material. The craft is what happens after.

A note on the examples: every before/after pair below was written for this document. None quotes an existing song. The "before" lines are deliberately typical — they are what a first draft (human or model) produces by default. That's the point: the default is the enemy.

**Using this skill** (for agents and humans alike): pick the register first (§4), draft against the craft canon (§1) inside the prosody budget (§2), then run everything through the rewrite protocol (§5) — which invokes the anti-slop gate (§3) as its blocking step. When a draft feels wrong and you can't name why, go to diagnostics (§6). The gate and the protocol are not advisory; a lyric that skipped them is a draft, whatever it calls itself.

## 1. The craft canon

Seven working methods, named honestly as influences — these are distillations of how the masters worked, not quotations of what they wrote.

### 1.1 The Cohen discipline: earn every line

Leonard Cohen famously ground songs through dozens of drafts over years. The usable rule is not "write eighty verses" — it's this: **a line stays only if it survives repeated re-reading without embarrassing you.** Most first-draft lines are placeholders wearing the costume of finished lines. Re-read the draft cold, out loud, a day later. Any line you skim past because you already know it's "fine" — that's the line to interrogate. "Fine" is the tell.

> **Before:** I'm broken and I'm lonely since the day you said goodbye
> **After:** Your coat is on the hook. I eat standing up.

The before-line reports a feeling. The after-line produces one, and it took three rewrites to find which two details did the work. Nothing in the after-line is poetic. All of it is specific.

Practical form: keep a **kill file**. Cut lines go there, not in the trash — a line wrong for this song is sometimes right for the next one. This makes cutting cheap, which makes it happen.

### 1.2 The Dylan chain: concrete images that don't explain themselves

Dylan's verses at their best are chains of photographable images with the connective tissue removed. The listener supplies the meaning, which is why the meaning lands — they built it. The usable rule: **when two images sit next to each other, delete the sentence between them that says what they mean.**

> **Before:** Everything is changing fast and nothing sacred stays the same
> **After:** They're auctioning the church pews / The auctioneer is my age

Do not chain more than the song can carry: a folk verse holds three or four images; a pop verse holds one or two. The chain fails when images become random — each image must be from the same *world* (the same town, the same night, the same argument), even when their logic is loose.

### 1.3 The Rilke turn: inwardness without abstraction

For the introspective register — the song about the self — the trap is abstraction ("finding myself," "who I really am"). Rilke's method: go *further in*, not further up. The inner life is rendered through its precise, almost embarrassing particulars, and through questions rather than conclusions. The usable rule: **an introspective line must contain either a question the singer can't answer or a detail only the singer would know.**

> **Before:** I need to find myself, discover who I'm meant to be
> **After:** I keep answering to a name / I'm not sure I ever chose

### 1.4 Blues compression: repetition that changes meaning

The AAB blues form — line, same line again, answer line — is the deepest compression technology in songwriting. The repeat is not padding: heard the second time, the line means more, because the listener now has time to feel it coming. The usable rule: **if you repeat a line, the repetition must do work — deepen it, darken it, or set up the turn.** If the second hearing adds nothing, you don't have a blues line, you have a stall.

> **Before:** I woke up in the morning and my baby she was gone away, and I felt so sad and lonely, I didn't know what I should do
> **After:** Woke up this morning — both pillows cold / Woke up this morning — both pillows cold / One of them was mine

Beyond twelve-bar forms, this compression instinct generalizes: say it once, shorter than feels safe, and trust the music to hold the space you didn't fill.

### 1.5 Hip-hop rhyme architecture: multisyllabic, internal, mosaic

The strongest rhyme tradition of the last fifty years runs on three mechanics: **internal rhyme** (rhymes inside the line, not just at the end), **multisyllabic rhyme** (two-plus syllables rhyming: *minimum / cinnamon*), and **mosaic rhyme** (several words rhyming against one: *make a mark / masquerade*... no — *rake the yard / take it hard*). The usable rule: **map the rhyme skeleton before judging a bar; a weak end-rhyme is forgivable if the internal architecture is dense, but sparse internals with a lazy end-rhyme is filler.**

> **Before:** I work hard every single day to try and get the fame / And everybody knows that soon they're gonna know my name
> **After:** Minimum wage in a maximum cage but the pages I fill in the margins are wages / Clock out, walk home through the rain with a name that the rain can't erase

Note what changed: *minimum/maximum*, *wage/cage/pages/wages*, *name/rain/erase* — the rhyme is load-bearing, and the images (margins of a notebook, walking home) survived the density. Density that kills the image is showing off; cut back until both live.

### 1.6 The Sondheim rules: prosody is law, content dictates form

Two non-negotiables from the musical-theater tradition at its most rigorous. First: **the natural spoken stress of a word must land on the musical stress.** A listener hears "re-MEM-ber"; if the melody forces "RE-mem-ber," the word breaks and takes the listener's trust with it. Second: **the form serves the content, never the reverse** — you don't pad a thought to fill a melody or amputate one to fit a rhyme scheme. If the thought is four syllables, the line is four syllables and the melody adjusts, or the thought gets a new line.

> **Before (stress-broken, sung on a strong-beat-first phrase):** to-DAY i de-CID-ed to LEAVE — *the melody stresses TO, DE, TO*
> **After:** PACKED the car at DAWN and DROVE — *every musical stress lands on a stressed syllable*

Corollary: **rhyme signals completed thought.** A perfect rhyme tells the ear "this idea just closed." Rhyming mid-thought, or perfect-rhyming a throwaway line, mis-signals. Use slant rhyme when the thought is still open.

### 1.7 The Van Zandt economy: cut the half that explains

Townes Van Zandt's lines are short because he deleted the explaining half. Most draft lines have two parts: the image and the caption. The usable rule: **find the caption and cut it.** The image, alone, at line length, with silence after it, does more.

> **Before:** The highway is so empty and it makes me feel so all alone
> **After:** The highway don't say nothing back

## 2. Prosody & singability

Lyrics are heard, not read. Every rule here exists because a singer's mouth and a listener's ear are physical systems.

### 2.1 Stress-matching

Before finalizing any line, **stress-map it**: mark the stressed syllables of natural speech, then mark where the musical strong beats fall (or will fall). They must coincide. The fastest check: speak the line in rhythm while tapping the beat. Any syllable you have to distort to make fit is a rewrite flag — rewrite the *words*, don't bend the delivery.

### 2.2 Vowels on held notes

A note held longer than a beat needs an **open vowel**: *ah, oh, ay, ee* (in descending order of power for belts and sustains — *ah/oh* carry, *ee* thins but cuts). Closed vowels (*ih* as in "sit," the schwa in "the") and vowels closed by consonants (*-it, -ick, -up*) collapse when sustained. AI vocal models fail on this even harder than humans do: a hook whose long note lands on "it" will synthesize as a smear. Design the hook so the money note lands on an open-vowel word — and if the right word has the wrong vowel, change the melody's landing point or find the synonym.

### 2.3 Consonant clusters at speed

Above roughly 120 BPM with dense syllables, consonant clusters jam: *"crisp scripts stack"* is unsingable at pace and un-synthesizable at any pace. Watch for **s-cluster pileups** (*-sts, -sks, str-*), adjacent stops (*"black cat took"*), and word boundaries that fuse (*"want to* → *wanna"* is fine; *"lost stars"* → *"lo-stars"* is not, unless you want it). Liquids and nasals (*l, r, m, n*) flow at any tempo — this is why they dominate every language's lullabies.

### 2.4 Line length vs tempo

Syllable budget scales inversely with BPM. Working bands (aligned with the vibe-os tempo bands and the Arcanea CANON bands):

| Tempo band | Context (vibe-os / Arcanea) | Syllables per line (one bar of melody) |
|---|---|---|
| 50–70 | meditation / deep-calm | 3–6, long vowels, generous rests |
| 60–80 | relaxation / contemplative-mythic (Arcanea 70–90 overlaps here) | 5–8 |
| 80–100 | focus / mid-tempo verse | 8–12 |
| 100–120 | moderate energy / heroic (Arcanea 90–130) | 10–14, or halve for punch |
| 120–145 | high energy / battle (Arcanea 130–160) | short declarative lines 5–8, or committed double-time 14+ |
| 145+ | maximum drive | hooks and chants only; verses go double-time or instrumental |

These are budgets, not targets. A 90 BPM verse with 6-syllable lines and space is a *choice* (a strong one); a 90 BPM verse with 15-syllable lines is a *mistake*.

### 2.5 Where the title sits

The title is the song's handle; place it where the hand reaches. Defaults by tradition: **pop** — title inside the chorus, sung within the first 60 seconds; **country** — title as the chorus's payoff line, ideally recontextualized by the final chorus; **hip-hop** — title as the chant/refrain anchor; **ballads** — title may sit at the top of the chorus and again as the last sung line. Whatever the placement, the title's stressed syllable should own the strongest beat of its phrase. A title never sung is a decision — make it deliberately or not at all.

### 2.6 Breath

Singers breathe; melodies that leave nowhere to breathe get their phrases amputated live and their consonants swallowed in the booth. The rule of thumb: **no more than two bars of continuous syllables without a rest or a held vowel** (a hold is a breath opportunity for the next phrase's onset). When a lyric reads perfectly but sings breathlessly, the fix is almost always the Van Zandt cut (§1.7), not a faster delivery. AI vocal models don't breathe, but they trained on singers who do — over-dense phrasing degrades synthesis the same way it degrades performance.

## 3. The anti-slop gate

This gate is **mandatory and blocking**: a lyric that fails any check goes back to rewrite, it does not ship with a note. The gate exists because generative drafts (and tired human drafts) converge on the same reassuring mush, and the mush is detectable.

### 3.1 The banned-cliché list

These words and constructions are banned outright in shipped lyrics — not because the words are evil, but because in a lyric they are almost always a placeholder where an image should be:

- **neon** (any usage — the single strongest AI-lyric tell)
- **echoes** / *echoes in the dark* / *echoes of ___*
- **whispers of ___** / *the wind whispers*
- **tapestry** (of anything)
- **symphony of ___**
- **unleash / unleashed**
- **journey** as a metaphor for life; *journey of a thousand ___*
- generic empowerment: *rise up, stand tall, break these chains, we are unstoppable, find your fire, born to fly, touch the sky, reach for the stars*
- **shadows dance / shadows fall** as scene-setting
- **fire inside / burning in my soul / this flame within**
- **city lights** as an emotion substitute
- **chasing dreams / chase the ___**
- **hollow / empty inside** as self-report
- **storm inside me / weather the storm**
- **heart of gold / heart on my sleeve** and other pre-owned heart idioms
- **meant to be / written in the stars**
- *in the silence* as a line opener
- **soul** used as an intensifier (*soul on fire, deep in my soul*)

Escape clause: a banned item may appear only if it is being *used against itself* (irony, subversion, a character who talks in clichés) — and that use must be flagged in the gate report, not smuggled.

### 3.2 Abstract-noun density limit

Count the abstract nouns: *love, hope, fear, pain, dreams, heart* (non-anatomical), *soul, truth, faith, time* (unanchored), *forever, memories, feelings*. **Limit: one per four lines in verses.** The chorus may carry one abstract noun as its center of gravity *if the verses have already paid for it* with concrete detail. Abstractions are conclusions; verses are evidence. A song that is all conclusions convinces no one.

### 3.3 The "could any song say this?" test

Take each line and ask: *could this line appear, unchanged, in a thousand other songs?* If yes, cut or rewrite. "I miss you more than words can say" belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one. The fix is always the same move: swap the universal claim for the particular evidence. Not "I miss you" — *what did you do at 7:40 this morning because they're gone?*

### 3.4 The concrete-detail quota

**Every verse must contain at least one image you could photograph.** Not a mood, not a claim — a thing: an object, a gesture, a place at a time of day. One is the floor, not the ceiling. The photographable detail is what makes a listener trust everything else in the verse; it is the lyric's proof of presence.

### 3.5 The third-thing rule

Never write about the emotion by naming the emotion; write about the **third thing** — the object the emotion passes through. The lovers via the kitchen table they bought together. The grief via the phone contact you can't delete. The ambition via the shoes worn through at the toe. The listener feels the current only when there's a wire. If a draft addresses its subject head-on ("our love," "my pain"), find the third thing and rebuild the verse around it.

### 3.6 Gate output

Running the gate produces a short report: each check, pass/fail, and for every fail the offending line and a rewrite direction. "Slop-gate: passed" with no receipts is itself a fail.

## 4. The register system

Three registers. The register is chosen *before drafting*, driven by the brief — and for Arcanea-label work, the register is canon-constrained, not a preference.

### 4.1 English verse

Everything in sections 1–3 applies at full strength. Genre calibrates the dials; POV and tense are chosen deliberately and kept consistent. This is the default register when no label canon says otherwise.

| Genre | Rhyme density | Rhyme type bias | Imagery load | Title placement | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hip-hop | High | Internal, multisyllabic, mosaic | High, urban-particular | Chant/refrain anchor | Rhyme skeleton is the structure (§1.5) |
| Country | Mid | Perfect on payoff lines, slant elsewhere | High, narrative, domestic | Chorus payoff line | Story must resolve; final chorus recontextualizes the title |
| Pop | Mid | Perfect in chorus, free in verse | 1–2 strong images per verse | In chorus, sung inside 60s | Chorus = the one transportable idea, nothing else |
| Folk / singer-songwriter | Low | Slant, or none | Very high, chained (§1.2) | Optional; may never be sung | The images carry what rhyme would elsewhere |
| Rock | Low–mid | Loose | Mid, gestural | First or last line of chorus | Prosody over rhyme — the line must shout well |
| Blues | Structural (AAB) | Perfect on the B-line | High, bodily, domestic | The A-line often *is* the title | Repetition does the work (§1.4) |
| Ballad / torch | Low | Perfect at section closes | High, intimate | Top of chorus + final sung line | Held-note vowel design (§2.2) is critical |

### 4.2 Arcanea mythic: languageless and invented-syllable

Arcanea's vocal canon (per the label CANON: choral, languageless, mythic-cadence — **clean English-lyric pop vocals are refused at gate**) means the lyricist's job shifts from semantics to **phoneme design**. The craft does not relax; it relocates.

**Designing a singable syllable inventory:**

- Build from **open vowels** (*a, e, o* — *ah* and *oh* dominant) and **liquid/nasal consonants** (*l, r, m, n*) with sparing stops (*t, k, d*) for rhythmic articulation. *Vela, oran, ame, lira, soleh* sing; *skrit, blects, strap* do not.
- **Small inventory, deep reuse:** 6–12 distinct syllables per piece. The ear needs recurrence to hear structure instead of babble. Invented language earns belief the same way a melody does — by coming back.
- **Syllable prosody still applies:** stressed invented syllables on strong beats, open vowels on holds. §2 is register-independent.
- **Contour rule:** rising phrases end on open vowels (*-a, -o*); grounding phrases may close with nasals (*-en, -am, -un*). This is how languageless lines still carry grammar-like motion.

**Motif-syllables per Guardian:** each Guardian persona should own a small recurring syllable cell — its sonic signature across releases — *derived from the Guardian's name and canonical voice character* in the label CANON (e.g., a Guardian named Alera, voice "musical, resonant, harmonic," naturally yields an *a-le / e-ra / lu-na*-family cell; a shadow-register Guardian with "whispered + processed" posture wants closed, darker cells — *un, mor, veh*). Derive, don't invent against the canon; the roster table and per-Guardian vocal character in `Starlight-Intelligence-System/verticals/music-is/labels/arcanea/CANON.md` are authoritative. Frequency-canon language (528 Hz, Solfeggio) stays on Arcanea surfaces only, per the brand-register rule in `BRAND-MAP.md`.

**Worked example — designing a Guardian cell.** Take a hypothetical shadow-register Guardian: canonical voice "whispered + processed, introspective, truthful," realm dark, tempo band contemplative (70–90 BPM). Derivation, step by step:

1. **Harvest phonemes from the canon.** The Guardian's name and realm words supply the raw sounds; keep the ones that match the vocal character. Whispered delivery favors nasals, soft fricatives, closed vowels: *n, m, v, sh, u, o(closed), eh*.
2. **Build the cell — 3 core syllables, 3 satellites.** Core (the signature, recurs every release): *nu — veh — mor*. Satellites (this piece only): *ish, ona, ehn*. Six total, inside the 6–12 inventory rule.
3. **Assign grammar-like roles.** *nu* opens phrases (short, unstressed pickup); *veh* carries stressed mid-phrase beats (singable *eh*, soft onset); *mor* closes grounding phrases (nasal ending, dies well in a whisper). Satellites decorate; core structures.
4. **Test at tempo.** 78 BPM, whispered: "nu-veh-mor... nu-veh, ish-ona-mor" — no cluster jams, stresses land on *veh* and *mor*, the held note (if any) goes to *ona*'s open *ah*, the only open vowel in the set — which makes it the ceiling of the piece by construction. One open vowel in a dark inventory is a design feature: scarcity makes the lift audible.
5. **Log the cell.** The core three syllables get recorded with the persona so the next release reuses them — recurrence across releases is what turns a syllable cell into a signature.

Contrast a bright, resonant Voice-Guardian: same method, opposite harvest — open vowels dominant (*a, e, o*), liquids (*l, r*), core cell like *a-le / ru / na*, holds available on every core syllable. The method is register-independent; the canon supplies the palette.

**Quasi-Latin: when it works, when it's kitsch.** It works as *texture*: real liturgical-register single words (*lumen, terra, aeterna, sanctus*-adjacent vocabulary) used as isolated sonic objects, never assembled into pretend sentences. It becomes kitsch the moment it fakes grammar — invented declensions, English words in Latin costume (*"epicus maximus"*), or strung-together dictionary words posing as a prayer. The test: **would a listener who knows Latin wince?** If the piece needs more than isolated words, cross fully into invented syllables — a language that belongs to no one can't be wrong.

### 4.3 Hybrid

Two proven shapes:

1. **English verses, mythic chorus** — the verses carry narrative in full §1–3 discipline; the chorus lifts into languageless ensemble. The seam works when the last English line *hands off* an open vowel into the choral entry.
2. **Anchor line** — one English line (gate-passed, concrete, short) recurring inside an otherwise languageless piece. One line means *one*: the second English line converts the piece to English-register expectations and the canon gate may refuse it.

For Arcanea releases, hybrid is only available where the Guardian's canon posture allows lead vocal (e.g., a Voice-Guardian persona); default Arcanea remains fully languageless. When in doubt, the Guardian brief decides, and the human confirms.

## 5. The rewrite protocol

Lyrics ship only after this sequence. No step is skippable; steps 2–4 loop until clean.

1. **Draft.** Full lyric, fast, judgment off. The draft's only job is to exist.
2. **Read aloud.** In rhythm, at tempo. Mark every stumble, jam, and distorted syllable. (Mythic register: sing the syllables, same test.)
3. **Stress-map.** Per §2.1 — speech stress vs musical stress, line by line. Rewrite words, not delivery. Check held-note vowels (§2.2) and cluster jams (§2.3).
4. **Slop-gate.** Run §3 in full, produce the report with receipts. Any fail → rewrite the offending lines → back to step 2 for those lines.
5. **Cut 20%.** By syllable count or line count, the draft loses roughly a fifth. This is not optional trimming — it is the forcing function that finds the captions (§1.7), the stalls (§1.4), and the third verse that was really the second verse again. If the cut genuinely breaks the song, restore the minimum and record why.
6. **The Cohen question.** Read the survivor cold and ask of the whole: *is this true?* Not factually — emotionally. Does the singer of this lyric exist? Would they say these words, in this order, meaning them? If any section reads as performance of a feeling rather than the feeling, it goes back to step 1 for that section. This is the only subjective step, and it is the most important one.
7. **Final format.** Only now: section tags, delivery notes, destination formatting (AI-generation package or human chart), and the output contract fields the consuming agent requires.

The protocol's cost is real — a lyric takes three to five passes instead of one. That cost is the product. Anyone can have the first draft; the first draft is what everyone already has.

### 5.1 Worked example: one quatrain through the protocol

Brief: mid-tempo (92 BPM) breakup verse, first person, present tense, English register. **Step 1, the draft:**

> I'm sitting in the darkness with the memories of you
> My heart is broken open and I don't know what to do
> The echoes of your laughter still are ringing in my head
> I'm drowning in the silence of this cold and empty bed

**Step 2–3, read aloud + stress-map:** line 3 jams — "still are ringing" forces a stress on "are" (a function word) to keep the meter; "-ness with the mem-" in line 1 smears at tempo. **Step 4, slop-gate report:** *echoes* — banned (§3.1). *silence* as scene-filler — flagged (§3.1). Abstract-noun count: *darkness, memories, heart, echoes, silence* = five in four lines against a limit of one (§3.2). "My heart is broken... I don't know what to do" — fails "could any song say this?" (§3.3). Photographable images: zero; "cold and empty bed" is the nearest miss but it's a stock phrase, not an observation (§3.4). Third thing: absent — the verse addresses the breakup head-on (§3.5). Verdict: total rewrite; find the third thing first. The third thing chosen: the other person's habits still running in the apartment. **Rewrite, then step 5, cut 20%:**

> Your alarm goes off at seven — I let it play through
> Two mugs out of habit, I pour one down the drain
> Your side of the bed stays made now
> I sleep on my side anyway

**Step 6, the Cohen question:** the alarm nobody turns off, the second mug — does this singer exist? Yes: still keeping the routines because ending them is the real goodbye. The last line was originally "I still sleep on my side, like you're coming back someday" — the caption got cut (§1.7); "anyway" carries it. **Gate re-run:** abstract nouns: zero. Photographable images: four. Banned list: clean. Stress-map: "seven," "habit," "made," "side" all land on strong beats at 92 BPM; the held note goes to "play" — open *ay*, safe to sustain. Ships.

Notice the shape of the fix: not better adjectives on the old lines — a different *object* for the song to look at. That is the usual shape.

## 6. Diagnostics: common failure modes

When a draft feels wrong but you can't name why, check these in order. Each names the disease, the tell, and the fix from the sections above.

| Failure mode | The tell | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Rhyme-forced line** | The line exists only because it rhymes ("I felt the rain / it caused me pain") | Rewrite from the *thought*, take slant rhyme (§1.6), or move the rhyme word |
| **Caption disease** | Lines that explain the image they just gave | Van Zandt cut (§1.7) — delete the explaining half |
| **Verse 2 = Verse 1 again** | Same information, new wording; the story hasn't moved | Verse 2 must advance time, stakes, or knowledge — or the song is one verse long, which is allowed |
| **Chorus doesn't lift** | Chorus is more verse — narrative detail instead of the transportable idea | Chorus carries the *conclusion* (its one permitted abstraction, §3.2); move the evidence back to the verses |
| **Bridge as filler** | Bridge restates the chorus at a different pitch | The bridge changes the camera angle: new POV, time jump, the counter-argument, or cut it |
| **Mood-board verse** | Images are vivid but from different worlds; nothing accumulates | Dylan chain constraint (§1.2): one world per verse — same night, same room, same argument |
| **Slogan anthem** | Every line addresses the crowd; no singer exists | Rebuild from one person's particulars (§1.3, §3.5) — the anthem effect comes from the crowd recognizing itself, not from being addressed |
| **Babble (mythic register)** | Invented syllables never recur; structure inaudible | Shrink the inventory, reuse the core cell (§4.2) — recurrence is the grammar |
| **Wince Latin (mythic register)** | Quasi-Latin assembled into pretend sentences | Isolated texture words or full invention — nothing in between (§4.2) |
| **Reads well, sings badly** | Great on the page, stumbles at tempo | You wrote a poem. Stress-map (§2.1), vowel check (§2.2), breath check (§2.6) — rewrite words, not delivery |
